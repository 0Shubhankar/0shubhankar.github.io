Emergency Vehicle Simulation

In the bustling arteries of urban landscapes, the efficient navigation of emergency vehicles can mean the difference between life and death. This simulation leverages the SUMO (Simulation of Urban Mobility) framework, an open-source, portable, microscopic, and continuous multi-modal traffic simulation package designed to handle large networks. Developed by the German Aerospace Center and a community of users, SUMO allows for the exploration of innovative traffic management solutions aimed at enhancing the response times of emergency services. By simulating the impact of dynamically controlled traffic signals at various distances—200 meters and 1500 meters—this research assesses how strategic light control can significantly reduce delays. Additionally, employing Dijkstra’s algorithm allows for the real-time computation of the quickest routes, further optimizing the speed and efficiency of emergency vehicles amidst complex city traffic. This introduction lays the groundwork for a detailed exploration into harnessing technology to save lives through smarter urban traffic management.

Signal Timing and Traffic Flow Efficiency

  • The simulation demonstrates two scenarios where traffic signals turn green when the ambulance is either 200 meters or 1500 meters from the signal. The results show that the ambulance takes significantly less time to reach its destination when the signal turns green at 1500 meters than at 200 meters.
  • This happens because in the 1500-meter scenario, the vehicles ahead of the ambulance have sufficient time to clear the road, enabling the ambulance to maintain a higher speed. In contrast, when the signal turns green at 200 meters, the vehicles in front of the ambulance are often unable to move in time, causing delays and reducing the average speed of the ambulance.

Representation of Vehicle Dynamics and Traffic Signal Control

  • Vehicle Departure Condition: A vehicle departs when its speed exceeds 0. Departure time, \( t_{\text{departure}} \), is noted when \( v_{\text{current}} > 0 \), where \( v_{\text{current}} \) denotes the vehicle's current speed.
  • Speed and Distance Updates: During each simulation step, the vehicle's speed, \( v_{\text{current}} \), is updated to \( v(t) \times 3.6 \) km/h, and the total distance covered, \( d_{\text{total}} \), is calculated as \( \frac{d(t)}{1000} \) kilometers, where \( v(t) \) and \( d(t) \) represent the speed in m/s and distance in meters, respectively.

Traffic Light Control Based on Proximity

  • Traffic signals change to green when the emergency vehicle is less than 200 meters away, and preemptively change if it's within 1500 meters, based on the vehicle's distance \( d_{\text{next_tls}} \) from the next traffic light.

Travel Time and Average Speed

  • Upon reaching the destination, the total travel time, \( t_{\text{travel}} \), is \( t_{\text{arrival}} - t_{\text{departure}} \), with \( t_{\text{arrival}} \) marking the arrival time.
  • The average speed, \( v_{\text{avg}} \), is calculated as \( \frac{d_{\text{total}}}{t_{\text{travel}} / 3600} \) km/h, where \( t_{\text{travel}} \) is in seconds.

Use of the Krauss Car-Following Model in Emergency Vehicle Simulation

The Krauss car-following model plays a key role in generating realistic traffic behavior around the emergency vehicle in this simulation. The model calculates how each vehicle adjusts its speed based on the distance to the vehicle in front. The following equation governs the model:

\[ v_{\text{next}} = \min \left( v_{\text{free}}, \max \left( v_{\text{safe}}, v_{\text{current}} - \frac{2 \times (s_{\text{current}} - s_{\text{desired}})}{T} \right) \right) \]

Where:

  • vnext: The vehicle's next speed.
  • vfree: The desired speed in free-flow conditions.
  • vsafe: The safe speed to avoid collisions, based on the distance to the vehicle ahead.
  • vcurrent: The current speed of the vehicle.
  • scurrent: The current distance to the vehicle ahead.
  • sdesired: The desired safe following distance.
  • T: The driver’s reaction time, usually around 1.5 seconds.

In the context of the emergency vehicle, this model helps simulate how other vehicles dynamically adjust their speeds to either make way or follow at a safe distance. It gives a realistic depiction of traffic flow around the emergency vehicle, helping us understand how speed and distance are influenced by the presence of the ambulance. This provides insight into potential delays and efficiency improvements in emergency response routing.

Dijkstra’s Algorithm: Shortest Path Optimization

Dijkstra’s algorithm is employed to dynamically calculate the shortest path for the emergency vehicle, ensuring that it takes the most efficient route based on real-time traffic conditions.

Graph Representation

The road network is modeled as a graph \( G=(V, E) \), where:

  • \( V \) is the set of vertices (nodes) representing intersections.
  • \( E \) is the set of edges representing roads between intersections, with each edge having a weight \( w(u, v) \), representing the distance or time between two nodes \( u \) and \( v \).

Algorithm Steps

  1. Initialization:
    • Set the distance of the starting node \( s \) to zero: \[ d(s)=0 \]
    • Set the distance of all other nodes to infinity: \[ d(v)=\infty \quad \forall v \in V, v \neq s \]
    • Add all nodes to a priority queue \( Q \), prioritized by their tentative shortest distance.
  2. Relaxation: For each node \( u \) being processed, check its neighboring nodes \( v \), and update the shortest path distance:

    \[ \text{If } d(u) + w(u, v) < d(v), \text{ update } d(v)=d(u) + w(u, v) \]

  3. Shortest Path Selection: Select the node \( u \) with the smallest tentative distance, and continue the process until all nodes have been processed.
  4. Termination: The process ends when the destination node \( t \) is reached, or all nodes have been processed.

Result: The algorithm returns the shortest path distance \( d(t) \) from the start node \( s \) to the destination node \( t \), ensuring the most efficient route for the emergency vehicle.

Traffic Signal Preemption Using TraCI

SUMO’s Traffic Control Interface (TraCI) allows real-time control of traffic lights based on the distance of the emergency vehicle to the next signal. The representation of signal preemption is as follows:

  1. Traffic Signal State:
    • Let \( S(t) \) denote the state of the traffic signal at time \( t \), where:
    • \( S(t)=\text{"R"} \) represents a red signal.
    • \( S(t)=\text{"Y"} \) represents a yellow signal.
    • \( S(t)=\text{"G"} \) represents a green signal.
  2. Signal Preemption Logic:
    • Preemptively change the signal to green if the ambulance is 1500 meters away:

      \[ \text{If } d_{\text{next\_tls}}(t) < 1500 \Rightarrow S(t)=\text{"G"} \]

    • Change the signal to green immediately if the ambulance is 200 meters away:

      \[ \text{If } d_{\text{next\_tls}}(t) < 200 \Rightarrow S(t)=\text{"G"} \]

Conclusion

The SUMO simulation provides vital insights into optimizing emergency vehicle response times through advanced traffic signal preemption and route optimization using Dijkstra’s algorithm. By adapting traffic signals based on the proximity of an emergency vehicle, the simulation underscores the potential for significant reductions in travel times and enhancements in service efficiency. However, the current model's limitations highlight the need for integrating more dynamic and context-aware systems that consider traffic density, real-time incidents, and alternative routing options to address complex urban traffic scenarios. Future iterations could benefit from leveraging real-time traffic data and machine learning techniques to predict and manage urban traffic flows more effectively, thereby offering a more robust and reliable framework for emergency response operations in urban environments.

Limitations of the Simulation

  • Context-Insensitive Traffic Control: The current simulation relies predominantly on distance metrics to manage traffic signals, overlooking critical factors such as the actual traffic density, intersection activity, and emergency vehicle type. This approach could lead to inefficient traffic management under varying urban conditions, where dynamic factors play a significant role.
  • Lack of Real-Time Adaptability: Dijkstra’s algorithm, while effective for static route calculation, does not inherently adapt to sudden changes such as road closures or traffic accidents. This limitation necessitates supplementary systems that can integrate updates in real-time to provide truly adaptive routing options for emergency responses.
  • Simulation of Traffic Conditions: The simulation presets traffic scenarios that may not accurately mirror the unpredictable nature of real-world traffic patterns. The lack of variability in traffic conditions can skew the results, reducing the applicability of the findings to real-world settings where traffic behavior is highly irregular.
  • Simplification of Emergency Vehicle Behavior: The model assumes that emergency vehicles strictly adhere to the shortest computed routes. In reality, the decision-making process for routing emergency vehicles is more complex and may involve spontaneous decisions to use alternative pathways such as bus lanes, which are not accounted for in the simulation.
  • Limited Computational Resources: The complexity and scale of the simulation demand substantial computational power. Limited resources can slow down the simulation process, potentially affecting the timeliness and resolution of results, which is critical for a real-time application like traffic management and emergency response optimization.
