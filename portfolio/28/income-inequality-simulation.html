Hover over each cluster to see detailed information about the movement of income groups. This feature reveals how changes in income levels affect population dynamics, including the number of individuals moving in and out of clusters and the resulting socio-economic distribution.

This simulation models and visualizes the dynamics of population movement within a city, influenced by varying socio-economic conditions. Individuals from different income groups tend to cluster together over time. Certain areas of the city become more concentrated with specific income groups, reflecting how income disparities can lead to divisions within urban spaces. Notably, those at the bottom of the income pyramid are more susceptible to income loss, leading to increased migration among this group. As the simulation runs, new urban patterns continuously evolve, showcasing how these migrations shape the city's landscape and further illustrate the complexities of socio-economic dynamics.

Explanation of the Simulation

Income Distribution: The total population \( P \) is divided into various income categories \( L \) with the distribution defined as:

\( n_k = \text{round}(P \cdot p_k) \)

where \( p_k \) is the proportion assigned to income category \( L_k \).

\( n_{\text{poor}} = \text{round}(P \cdot 0.15) \)

\( n_{\text{lowIncome}} = \text{round}(P \cdot 0.20) \)

\( n_{\text{lowerMiddleClass}} = \text{round}(P \cdot 0.25) \)

\( n_{\text{middleClass}} = \text{round}(P \cdot 0.25) \)

\( n_{\text{upperMiddleClass}} = \text{round}(P \cdot 0.10) \)

\( n_{\text{rich}} = \text{round}(P \cdot 0.05) \)

Cluster Setup: Each cluster \( C_i \) is defined by its coordinates \( (x_i, y_i) \) and assigned an "ease of living" factor \( E_i \), which encompasses job opportunities, amenities, and access to facilities:

\( E_i = f(\text{job opportunities}, \text{amenities}, \text{access to facilities}) \)

Population Initialization: Individuals \( j \) in income category \( k \) are randomly positioned within the bounds of their starting cluster \( C_k \):

\( (x_j, y_j) \in C_k \)

Income Change Dynamics: Each individual has a probability \( \rho \) to change their income level at each time step \( t \). This change can be represented as:

\( \text{incomeChange} = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{with probability } \frac{1}{2} \\ -1 & \text{with probability } \frac{1}{2} \end{cases} \)

The new income level index \( \text{newIndex} \) is computed as:

\( \text{newIndex} = \text{currentIndex} + \text{incomeChange} \)

Movement Based on Income Change and Ease of Living: If an individual's income increases (\( L_k \to L_{k+1} \)), they seek a cluster with a higher ease of living:

\( C_{\text{target}} = \arg \max_{C_i} (E_i \mid E_i > E_{\text{current}}) \)

Conversely, for a decrease:

\( C_{\text{target}} = \arg \min_{C_i} (E_i \mid E_i < E_{\text{current}}) \)

This movement is influenced by changes in income. If an individual's income increases, they seek to relocate to a cluster with a higher ease of living factor than their current one. Conversely, if their income decreases, they look for more affordable options. Each month, the simulation checks for these income changes, considering how housing prices and rent associated with various locations relate to ease of living and an individual's ability to afford a place based on their income.

Simulation of Movement: Movement towards the target cluster's coordinates \( (x_{\text{target}}, y_{\text{target}}) \) is modeled as follows:

\( x_{j, \text{new}} = x_j + \alpha (x_{\text{target}} - x_j) \)

\( y_{j, \text{new}} = y_j + \alpha (y_{\text{target}} - y_j) \)

where \( \alpha \) is a constant determining the speed of movement.

Moving to the Target Cluster: The individual moves to the target cluster, adjusting their position by a random offset:

\( \text{targetX} = x_{\text{target}} + \epsilon \)

\( \text{targetY} = y_{\text{target}} + \epsilon \)

where \( \epsilon \) is a random value within a specified range.

Updating Cluster : When an individual moves to a new cluster, the process involves two key updates:

Remove the individual from the old cluster and increment the old income counter:

\( C_{\text{old}}(\text{oldIncome}) \gets C_{\text{old}}(\text{oldIncome}) + 1 \)

Add the individual to the new cluster and increment the new income counter:

\( C_{\text{new}}(\text{newIncome}) \gets C_{\text{new}}(\text{newIncome}) + 1 \)

This maintains accurate population counts in each cluster.

Limitations of the Simulation: While this simulation provides a dynamic visual representation of socio-economic impacts on urban population movement, it inherently simplifies complex human behaviors and economic conditions into quantifiable metrics. It does not fully account for the subtleties of human decision-making, the unpredictability of economic shifts, or the full spectrum of social factors that influence migration. Furthermore, the model assumes static "ease of living" factors for clusters, which in reality are dynamic and can change due to various external influences. The simulation also relies on predefined income changes and does not consider other crucial factors such as family dynamics, cultural ties, or personal preferences, which significantly impact residential choices. As such, while useful for illustrating general trends, the simulation may not accurately predict individual or group behavior in real-world scenarios. For more robust predictions and analyses, integrating more variables and employing a more sophisticated modeling approach to capture the nuances of urban dynamics and individual socio-economic conditions would be beneficial.

Conclusion

This visualization offers a structured framework for understanding how individuals navigate and relocate within urban environments in response to economic changes. By incorporating income categories and dynamic income variations, the model captures the intricate patterns of urban migration, emphasizing how socio-economic factors influence population distribution. As individuals experience fluctuations in income, their decisions about where to live are affected by the characteristics of various clusters, such as job opportunities, access to amenities, and overall quality of life. This reflects the complex interplay between economic status and living conditions, illustrating that migration is not just a response to income but also to the broader context of urban infrastructure and community dynamics. Analyzing this visualization provides valuable insights into how cities evolve over time, as certain areas become more desirable based on their economic attributes.
