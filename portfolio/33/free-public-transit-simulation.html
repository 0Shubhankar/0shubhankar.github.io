Imagine a city offers free bus rides as a public good. In this situation, the city needs some people to contribute (e.g., by paying taxes or volunteering) to keep the buses running. However, not everyone contributes. People in this simulation fall into three categories:

1. Cooperators (Blue): These people pay their fair share (either through taxes, donations, or other contributions). Their contribution helps keep the buses running, providing a public good for everyone. In the simulation, cooperators are represented as blue points, and their payoff depends on how much the bus system benefits from the contributions of all cooperators.

2. Defectors (Red): These people ride the buses without contributing. They benefit from the free service but don’t pay for it. In the simulation, defectors are red points. They free-ride on the public transport system without paying, benefiting from the buses without helping to sustain them.

3. Loners (White): These people don’t participate in the bus system at all. Instead of taking the bus, they might walk, cycle, or use private transport. They don’t contribute to or benefit from the bus system, but they also don’t get caught up in the collapse of the system if too many people defect. In the simulation, loners are white points. They receive a fixed payoff (representing the payoff from using alternatives, such as walking or using a car).

In the free bus ride scenario, the behavior of cooperators, defectors, and loners reflects different strategies that people might adopt when dealing with public goods like free transport.

Cooperators' Payoff: The payoff for cooperators depends on how much benefit they receive from a well-functioning bus system. The more cooperators there are, the more effective the system becomes, and the payoff increases.

Defectors' Payoff: Defectors get to use the bus system without contributing. They enjoy the benefits of free rides without paying taxes or contributing in other ways. However, if too many people defect, the bus system collapses, and the payoff decreases.

Loners' Payoff: Loners don't use the bus system at all. Instead, they walk or use private transport, receiving a fixed payoff.

Let's say we're simulating a free bus system in a city:

The cooperators represent people who contribute to the free bus system by paying taxes or supporting it financially. They benefit because a high number of contributors makes the bus system more reliable and efficient. The defectors represent people who use the free bus system but avoid paying taxes or contributing in any way. They still benefit from the service, but if too many people defect, the system might collapse or become unreliable. The loners represent people who don't use the bus system at all. They either walk, bike, or use their cars. They have a stable payoff because they don't rely on the public good, but they miss out on any benefits if the bus system is highly efficient and beneficial.

publicGoodRewardMultiplier (r): In the free bus ride scenario, this represents how effective and valuable the bus system is for those who use it. A high r means that the bus system is very beneficial (e.g., fast, reliable, and convenient). A low r means the bus system is not very effective, reducing the incentive to cooperate.

lonerFixedPayoff (σ): This represents the payoff loners receive by opting out of the bus system and using alternative transportation. It could represent walking or private transport. The higher the σ, the more attractive it is to be a loner and avoid the public good entirely.

interactionRadius: This parameter controls how close agents need to be to interact and form connections. In real life, this could represent how connected people are within neighborhoods or city districts. A larger interaction radius means more people are interacting and influencing each other (e.g., more interconnected neighborhoods).

Number of Cooperators, Defectors, and Loners: These control how many people adopt each strategy. In the free bus ride scenario, this represents how many people choose to cooperate (by supporting the bus system), defect (by using it without supporting it), or opt out entirely (by using alternative transportation).

In the simulation, agents representing cooperators, defectors, and loners are distributed on a 3D grid. As the simulation runs, the agents rotate in 3D space, and lines are drawn between cooperators and defectors when they are within a certain distance of each other (the interactionRadius). After each full rotation, the system updates the payoffs for each agent type based on their interactions and displays these results on the screen.

This code provides a visual simulation of a public good scenario (like a free bus ride system) using 3D graphics. The mathematical models behind the simulation reflect the payoffs for different strategies (cooperation, defection, or opting out). By adjusting the parameters in the GUI (such as the public good reward multiplier, the loner payoff, and the number of agents), you can explore how different scenarios play out in this simplified, but visually engaging, simulation.
