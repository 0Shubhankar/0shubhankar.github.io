Urban economics / public goods

The Free Bus Ride Dilemma

Free public transport raises a familiar economic question: who contributes to a service that others can use without paying? This experiment uses a small public-goods game to examine the difference between individual incentives and collective outcomes.

Participation and strategy

People follow one of three fixed strategies. Cooperators (blue) contribute one unit toward the service. Defectors (red) take part but do not contribute. Loners (white) choose an outside option with a fixed payoff. The spheres show these groups. The connecting lines indicate visual proximity; they do not determine who shares a payoff.

How the model works

Let \(C\), \(D\), and \(L\) be the numbers of cooperators, defectors, and loners. The rider population is \(N=C+D\). Each payoff interaction brings together \(n\) riders selected at random, without replacement. If \(k\) of those riders cooperate, the group produces a benefit \(rk\) that is shared equally among its \(n\) members.

\[ b(k,n)=\frac{rk}{n} \]

Here \(r\) is the public-good multiplier. A cooperator pays a normalized contribution cost of one unit. A defector pays nothing. A loner does not join the group and instead receives the outside-option payoff \(\sigma\).

Expected payoffs

The number of other cooperators in a randomly assembled group is uncertain. The model therefore calculates an expected payoff for each strategy. A cooperator meets, on average, \((n-1)(C-1)/(N-1)\) other cooperators. A defector meets \((n-1)C/(N-1)\). The focal person's own strategy changes the expected composition of the remaining group.

\[ \pi_C=\frac{r}{n}\left(1+\frac{(n-1)(C-1)}{N-1}\right)-1 \]

\[ \pi_D=\frac{r}{n}\frac{(n-1)C}{N-1},\qquad \pi_L=\sigma \]

These expressions apply when the corresponding strategy is present and \(N>1\). For a single rider, the contribution term is evaluated directly; a strategy with no members is shown as unavailable. The results are measured in abstract payoff units, not rupees, ticket prices, or measured welfare.

Reading the experiment

Use the controls to change the composition of the rider population and the size of a typical group. Smaller groups can make one person's contribution more consequential to their own return. Increasing \(r\) raises the collective value of cooperation. Increasing \(\sigma\) makes the outside option more attractive, but it does not change the benefits shared by riders.

These are expected outcomes from random matching, not observations of how people actually travel. Strategies do not evolve automatically, and the visual proximity network is separate from the game-theoretic calculations. Real transit funding also depends on taxation, fare policy, service capacity, and political institutions. The model isolates one incentive problem; it does not forecast ridership or assess a city's transport budget.
