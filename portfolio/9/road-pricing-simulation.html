Singapore's Electronic Road Pricing (ERP) system, introduced in 1998, was the world's first electronic toll collection system designed to manage urban congestion through road pricing. The system uses a network of gantries equipped with sensors and cameras to monitor and charge vehicles entering congested zones during peak hours, helping reduce traffic congestion in the city.

The success of the ERP system lies in its ability to adjust charges based on real-time traffic conditions, encouraging motorists to alter their travel times or routes. Each vehicle is fitted with an In-Vehicle Unit (IVU) that works with a stored-value card to ensure a seamless and efficient toll collection process, eliminating the need for physical toll booths.

Singapore's ERP system has proven to be effective, leading to a reduction in traffic congestion and improvements in air quality, especially in the central areas. The system enjoys widespread public acceptance, partly due to the government's reinvestment of toll revenues into public transportation and infrastructure improvements.

However, cities like New York have faced challenges in implementing similar congestion pricing systems. New York's recent efforts have been delayed due to concerns over privacy, the economic impact on commuters, and perceived fairness. A strong car culture and concerns about personal freedom have contributed to public resistance.

The successful implementation of congestion pricing schemes in other cities depends on critical factors such as comprehensive planning, effective public communication, investment in public transportation alternatives, adaptive pricing models, and addressing privacy and security concerns proactively.

As urban populations grow and traffic congestion becomes a global issue, Singapore's ERP system offers valuable lessons for other cities facing similar challenges.

This interactive map-based simulation allows users to visualize vehicle trips passing through Singapore’s ERP system, providing insights into how congestion pricing works in the city. The simulation uses Leaflet, a popular JavaScript library, to display a map of Singapore and plot routes of vehicles as they travel through ERP sites.

Users can explore various settings through the control panel to adjust parameters such as the drawing interval, the time of day, and the number of vehicle trips. The drawing interval controls how fast paths are drawn on the map: smaller values make the simulation faster, while larger values slow it down. Users can simulate different times of day to see how ERP site intensity changes during peak and off-peak traffic hours, and the number of trips controls the traffic density by adjusting how many vehicle routes are drawn at once.

ERP sites, where vehicles are charged, are marked on the map. As a vehicle passes near one of these sites, a marker with a dollar symbol ($) briefly appears, representing a toll being collected. Each time this happens, the total revenue counter is automatically updated. The vehicle routes are drawn dynamically from pre-defined paths stored in the `shortest_paths.json` file, and these paths fade away over time, creating a smooth visual effect that mimics the trails left by vehicles as they move.

The simulation also includes a heatmap layer that shows the intensity of traffic at ERP sites. The heatmap’s intensity changes based on the time of day, reflecting real-world congestion patterns. During peak hours, such as in the morning and evening, ERP sites have higher intensity, and more vehicles are likely to be charged.

As the simulation runs continuously, it adjusts the heatmap and vehicle routes in real-time, allowing users to explore how congestion pricing adapts to changing traffic conditions. By tweaking the simulation settings, users can observe how different traffic conditions impact the ERP system and its revenue generation.

In summary, this simulation provides an engaging way to understand how congestion pricing works in a city like Singapore, where vehicles traveling through ERP sites are charged based on traffic conditions. It offers a real-world example of how such systems can manage traffic flow and generate revenue in urban areas.
